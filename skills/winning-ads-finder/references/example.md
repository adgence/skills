## 1. The verdict

- **Paid demand is proven (High).** 34 of the 45 in-niche ads have run 60+ days, from 16 different brands. Several sellers keep paying for this niche; you're not testing whether people buy magnesium glycinate from ads.
- **The formula:** the long runners of 4 independent brands answer one question: *"why didn't my magnesium work?"* Their answer is the form, the dose or the fillers (oxide vs glycinate, "half the dose", "check the label"). They've run 167 to 261 days. Buyers' own questions in the library say the same thing: which form, which brand, is it fake.
- **Make first:** a label-check ad. Your gummy next to "a typical capsule", three facts a buyer can verify on your label. It's the niche's proven structure, without the named-brand attack and fake doctors the long runners use.

**Over-bought:** "your magnesium is the wrong form" is now the category's default argument. Saying it again without proof is paying to be ignored. Your edge has to be a label people can check.

**Unbought (Low, a hypothesis):** men. One ad in the 45 speaks to men ("75% of men are missing this one min…", 57 days). Every long runner I read speaks to women, mostly 40+.

**The niche's objections, free:** 6 text-only "asking for a friend" posts ran as ads. 4 ask which brand to trust or where to buy one that isn't fake ("amazon is full of fakes"), 2 ask which form to buy, 1 asks whether it works at all (some ask two). Your first ads should answer these three.

## 5. Risky to copy

The "Dr." and PhD persona pages, the AI-looking presenter and product images, university and PubMed logos, "we tested 47" and "heavy metals in 90%" with no test shown, naming Nature Made, "Big Pharma's magnesium scam", "neurologist sister". They survive in the library; they put a new brand's ad account at risk (Meta health and personal-attributes policies, FTC endorsement rules).
